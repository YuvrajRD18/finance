# Research on US State Public Pension Underfunding

## A. The Public Pension Underfunding Gap

The 50 states in the United States are collectively facing a significant public pension underfunding crisis that threatens the retirement security of millions of public employees. According to recent estimates from the Pew Charitable Trusts and other financial research organizations, the total unfunded liability for state pension systems ranges from approximately $1.4 trillion to over $4 trillion, depending on the assumptions used for calculating future obligations and expected investment returns.

The variation in these estimates stems primarily from the discount rate assumptions used to calculate future liabilities. When using the assumed rates of return (typically around 7%), the gap appears smaller, but when using more conservative market-based rates, the shortfall expands dramatically. The American Enterprise Institute and other policy research organizations have argued that using market-based discount rates reveals a much larger crisis than official state reports suggest.

Several states face particularly severe underfunding situations. Illinois consistently ranks among the worst-funded state pension systems in the nation, with a funded ratio below 40%, meaning the state has less than 40 cents for every dollar it owes in future pension benefits. Kentucky, New Jersey, and Connecticut also struggle with funded ratios well below the 80% threshold that actuaries generally consider healthy.

The causes of this underfunding crisis are multifaceted. States have historically failed to make required annual contributions to their pension funds, essentially deferring costs to future generations. Investment losses during market downturns, particularly during the 2008 financial crisis, significantly depleted pension fund assets. Additionally, increasing life expectancy means retirees collect benefits for longer periods than initially projected, and generous benefit enhancements granted during economic booms have added to long-term liabilities.

The COVID-19 pandemic initially raised concerns about further strain on pension systems, but strong market performance in subsequent years helped many pension funds recover some ground. However, market volatility and inflation concerns continue to pose risks to pension fund investment portfolios, potentially widening the funding gap in the future.

## B. How State Governments Deal with the Pension Underfunding Gap

State governments have employed a variety of strategies to address their pension underfunding challenges, ranging from benefit reforms to revenue increases and structural changes to their pension systems.

### Benefit Reforms

Many states have implemented reforms to reduce future pension costs. These reforms typically affect new employees rather than current workers or retirees, whose benefits are often protected by state constitutions or contract law. Common benefit reforms include:

- Increasing the retirement age for new employees
- Requiring higher employee contribution rates
- Reducing cost-of-living adjustments (COLAs) for retiree benefits
- Transitioning new employees from traditional defined benefit plans to hybrid plans or defined contribution plans similar to 401(k) accounts

For example, Rhode Island implemented significant pension reform in 2011 under then-State Treasurer Gina Raimondo, which included suspending cost-of-living adjustments, raising the retirement age, and creating a hybrid pension plan combining elements of defined benefit and defined contribution plans.

### Increased State Contributions

Some states have committed to making larger annual contributions to their pension funds to reduce the unfunded liability over time. This approach requires either cutting spending in other areas or increasing revenues, which can be politically difficult.

### Tax Policy Changes to Address Pension Underfunding

Several states have implemented tax policy changes specifically to help fund their pension obligations. Here are notable examples:

**Illinois:** Illinois has faced one of the most severe pension crises in the nation. In 2011, the state temporarily increased its individual income tax rate from 3% to 5% and the corporate income tax rate from 4.8% to 7%, with a significant portion of the additional revenue directed toward pension obligations. While these temporary rates were later reduced, Illinois has continued to explore various revenue options to address its pension obligations. In 2020, voters rejected a proposed constitutional amendment that would have allowed for a graduated income tax structure, leaving the state to continue relying on its flat income tax and other revenue measures.

**New Jersey:** New Jersey has explored various tax measures to address its pension funding shortfall. The state has considered lottery revenue dedication, tax on millionaires, and other revenue-generating measures to help close the pension funding gap.

**Connecticut:** Connecticut implemented tax increases and devoted significant portions of its budget to pension contributions, though this has crowded out spending on other state services and programs.

**Kentucky:** Following severe underfunding in its pension systems, Kentucky explored various revenue options, including expanding sales tax to additional services and considering increased contributions from state employees to help shore up the pension systems.

### Other Approaches

States have also used other creative approaches to address pension underfunding:

- **Pension obligation bonds:** Some states have issued bonds to make lump-sum contributions to their pension funds, betting that investment returns will exceed the interest costs on the bonds. This strategy is controversial and can be risky if investment returns fall short of expectations.

- **Asset transfers:** Some states have transferred assets or dedicated specific revenue streams (such as lottery proceeds) to pension funds.

- **Legal challenges:** In some cases, states have attempted to reduce benefits for current workers or retirees, leading to legal challenges. Courts in different states have reached varying conclusions about what benefit changes are permissible under state law.

### Conclusion

The public pension underfunding crisis remains one of the most significant fiscal challenges facing state governments across the United States. With unfunded liabilities in the trillions of dollars, states must balance the need to honor commitments to public employees with competing demands on limited state resources. The solutions involve difficult trade-offs between raising taxes, cutting other services, and reforming pension benefits. The approaches taken by different states provide a range of models, but no single solution has emerged as universally effective, and the challenge is likely to remain a major policy issue for years to come.
